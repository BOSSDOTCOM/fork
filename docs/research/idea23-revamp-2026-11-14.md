## 1. The recommendation in brief

1. **Change what is evolved.** The first target should be the **time-management policy**, scored on
   a replay simulator built from Fork's own logged searches. It is the only target where a candidate
   can be scored in seconds, without noise from games, on a quantity that has already predicted a
   game result in this project (the node-share fit behind time management (b), +18.69 STC and
   +10.23 LTC).
2. **Second target: row-choice state functions for the network**, scored by how much of the
   network's measured error they explain. This is the largest possible gain and the most original
   output, but its real test is a retrain, so it comes after the method has proved itself.
3. **Third target: a regime calibration layer on the evaluation** (the queens-on finding). It is
   cheap to test in games, but idea 180 and R33 already cover most of it by hand.
4. **Change the fitness.** A deterministic screen ranks candidates and never decides. Games decide.
   Before any tokens are spent, the screen itself is tested against results Fork already has, and a
   headroom bound says whether there is anything to find.
5. **Change the scale.** The pilot tried 40 candidates. The published loops that found something
   used thousands. With a screen that costs seconds, 4,000 candidates fit in about two days and
   under the earlier 10 M token cap *(estimate)*.
6. **Add a control arm.** The same funnel fed by independent proposals with no evolution. Without
   it, a gain cannot be credited to the evolutionary loop.

My judgement of the chances *(estimate, low confidence)*: about 25-35% that the time-management
pilot ends with at least one change adopted at LTC, worth +3 to +8 Elo if it does. More than half of
the failure risk is settled before any token or GitHub stage is spent (stages A0 and A1 below).

Four lessons for the redesign:

1. **A screen may rank; it may not decide.** This is already the rule (DESIGN.md "Testing setup",
   owner 2026-11-12): offline rules never gate a test and never pick a value by themselves.
2. **A screen must be tested against known game results before it is used**, and a placebo must not
   be able to reproduce its signal.
3. **Anything that costs speed must be judged on the clock**, or at fixed nodes with the speed
   measured in the same games.
4. **Selected winners shrink.** The validation run's four best gains vanished on a set four times
   larger (DESIGN.md:2159-2182). Whatever selects candidates must not also report their size.

## 4. Design principles

**P1. Pick a target by its ratio of effect to resolution.** A target qualifies only if a plausible
candidate's effect is larger than what an affordable test can resolve. Design R's H1 bound is 6
nElo, about 3.6 Elo at STC (Elo/nElo 0.59-0.61). A region whose best candidates are worth 1 Elo
cannot be searched by any test Fork can afford, however good the loop.

**P2. The model writes structure; measurement sets constants.** A candidate is a short function
with named free coefficients. The evaluator fits the coefficients; the model never supplies a
number. This compares structures at their best, removes the path by which model memory reproduces
famous values (KB §9, Acher and Jézéquel), and matches the owner's rule that constants are measured
before shipping. Fitted values stay placeholders until games confirm them.

**P3. Give candidates information the baseline does not use.** The KB's clearest finding on search
design is that the search space and the hints in the prompt matter more than the search algorithm
(Gideoni et al. §4.1). Each region's input list is part of the experiment and is logged.

**P4. Aim at Fork's own measured weaknesses.** A model asked for a search heuristic mostly recalls
the textbook. Something nobody else has is more likely where the evidence is Fork's alone: its
network's residuals, its own iteration traces, its own games.

**P5. Many restarts, a control arm, and a size gate.** Independent islands rather than one long
line (KB: restarts beat longer runs; rankings flip between one seed and forty). A no-evolution arm
on the same funnel. Short programs preferred, with a hard size limit.

**P6. Spend games last and on few.** Tokens were the cheap part of the pilot (543,832 for 40
candidates). GitHub stages are the scarce part. The design moves the volume to the screen and
states, for each game test, the decision it can change and its cost.

## 5. What to evolve: three ranked targets

### Rank 1. The time-management policy (lane A)

**The region.** Two small pure functions in `src/search/time_budget.cpp`, each in residual form
(final = base × (1000 + correction) / 1000, clamped; a zero correction reproduces today's engine):

- the **stop rule**: after each finished iteration, a scale on the soft limit, from the iteration's
  signals. Today this is variant (b): stability and node share.
- the **allocation rule**: at the start of a move, the share of the remaining clock this move may
  use. Today this is a fixed division (30 assumed moves left, 3/4 of the increment, hard limit 4
  shares, a tenth in reserve), and every one of those values is an unmeasured placeholder
  (staleness audit, row P5).

The hard limit, the reserve and the Move Overhead stay in the fixed skeleton. A candidate can only
return a clamped scale, so no candidate can lose on time by construction.

**Why it ranks first.**

- **Measured headroom.** One fitted curve gave +18.69 STC and +10.23 LTC. The allocation constants
  were never measured. E2 found the shipped scale under-spends at long searches (0.83 and 0.79 of
  the plain spend at 12 M and 24 M nodes). The eval-trend design
  (`docs/research/eval-trend-tm/design.md`) reports that the move before a collapse is played fast
  under (b) (time ratio 0.51-0.60 against 0.81-0.90 with (b) off; it rests on 18-23 collapses per
  corpus, so UNVERIFIED).
- **An honest, cheap fitness exists.** A stop rule can be replayed on logged searches without
  searching again. `tools/measure/tm_curve.py` and `e2_tm_replay.py` already do this with nodes as
  the clock. A candidate then costs seconds, which makes a real evolutionary search affordable.
  FunSearch's own scope note names exactly these conditions: an efficient evaluator, rich scores
  and an isolated part to evolve (KB §9).
- **No speed confound.** The code runs once per iteration at the root. It cannot cost nps, so the
  failure that sank every richer network does not apply.
- **The root is the one place where expensive knowledge is free.** A time policy may read anything
  about the root position: material, queens on the board, the gap between the static evaluation
  and the search score, a descriptor from lane B. This opens a kind of rule I did not find in the
  KB: spending more time where Fork's own evaluation is measured to be unreliable (queens-on
  middlegames with level material are 2.4% of training positions but 23% of the gauntlet's deciding
  positions).
- **It does not compete with the tuner.** Time-management parameters stay out of SPSA (think tank
  decision 4), and the owner already named this formula the next possible target.
- **Many time controls for free.** The KB records that time management tuned at STC did not carry
  over to very long controls. The simulator can score every candidate at STC-shaped, LTC-shaped
  and TCEC-shaped budgets at once.

**What speaks against it.** Two hand ideas after (b) did not pay: root-gap time was H0 (-4.89 ±
7.03), and the score-fall replay showed no gain over (b). The remaining headroom in the stop rule
may be small. Stage A1 below measures that bound before anything is spent. The simulator is also
open-loop (§6.3).

### Rank 2. Row-choice state functions for the network (lane B, consumer B2)

**The region.** A function from (piece, square, the pawns, optionally the kings) to a small state
number, which selects which input row the piece uses. This is ideas.md 175 (state-folded rows),
whose tier A states were chosen by hand. The loop would propose and rank states.

**Why it ranks second.**

- It applies round 3's cost lesson directly: "Rows cost, finding does not" (the king-window
  machinery ran at 1.005 with every row suppressed and 0.784 with its rows). A state changes which
  row a piece uses and adds none.
- It aims at the largest measured weakness. Against SF19 from 200 advantaged UHO starts Fork won
  none; the edge is lost at a median of ply 23 with queens on in 146 of 198 games; opponents see
  +150 a median of 4.5-11.5 moves before Fork sees -150.
- The one input family of this kind that Fork has tested paid: pawn links, +46.09 at fixed nodes
  and +32.67 at STC at 0.895 nps.
- A hand-picked list of states is exactly the kind of small, guess-driven search space where a
  program search over thousands of candidates can find what a person would not try.

**What speaks against it.** The real test is a retrain: about 2.2 T4 GPU-hours per seed, seed noise
of about ±6 Elo (up to ±10-15 in a single-seed SPRT), so two seeds per arm and a same-recipe
control. The screen (how much of the network's residual a state explains) is unvalidated, and loss
has not predicted strength in Fork. So this lane can rank states cheaply but can confirm only two
or three.

### Rank 3. A regime calibration layer on the evaluation (lane B, consumer B1)

**The region.** A function from cheap position descriptors to a class, with one fitted scale per
class applied to the network's output (the form R33 already designs: e' = e × S_class / 1024).

**Why it is on the list.** The finding is measured and large: the search score's logistic scale is
59.6 cp with queens on against 74.1 with queens off in Fork's STC games (ratio 1.243, 1.212-1.275);
level queenless positions are 94% draws but scored as edges. A calibration term costs no speed, so
a fixed-node screen is a fair test.

**Why it ranks third.** Idea 180's head rescale (planned first by the owner) and R33 (order step
17) already test the same thing by hand. The loop would add only the search over class definitions.
The gap is flat at 20,000 nodes and appears at STC and LTC depth, so part of it is a property of
the search, which a static scale may not fix. And the next retrain can absorb a pure scale. Run it
only if idea 180's hand version gains.

### Not chosen

| Target | Why not now |
|---|---|
| Pruning and reduction rules | The offline proxy for them failed 5 of 5. Effects per rule are near the test's resolution. Their constants go stale with every net (staleness audit). The known rules are already queued by hand under design R at lower cost, and a model will mostly re-derive them from memory, which is an originality risk rather than a discovery. Revisit after lane A shows whether the loop adds anything, with O9's antithetic fixed-node screen. |
| Training-data selection rules | Each candidate needs a retrain, and the training-side levers look spent: 2.5x training -0.43 ± 9.44, class weights -7.60 (H0), idea 80 pooled -0.98. |
| Move ordering | Done: a null result. |

## 6. Lane A in detail: the fitness pipeline for time management

### 6.1 The trace corpus (built once, reused by every candidate)

- **Source:** Fork's own SPRT games, replayed as games (`tools/measure/game_replay.py`: one engine
  process per game, the hash table and histories carried from move to move), each search run far
  past the point where any policy could stop, with one `iteration_trace` row per finished
  iteration.
- **Budget classes:** STC-shaped (searches to about 4 M nodes: four times the soft share at 8+0.08
  on the runners' 2.4 M nodes/s; about 1,200 games of about 60 searches each) and LTC-shaped (to
  about 16 M nodes; about 400 games). These sizes are estimates; stage A0's noise floor decides
  whether they are enough. A TCEC-shaped class
  (increment 1/600 of the base, the KB's 60+0.1 inference, and deeper searches) is left to the
  scale-up.
- **Signals per iteration:** today's fields (depth, best move, share, share over every pass,
  runner-up, nodes, score, passes, root moves, selective depth), plus fields to add: the scores of
  the previous iterations, aspiration fail-lows and fail-highs, the root's static evaluation, the
  root position (so descriptors can be computed), move number and clock state.
- **Reference labels:** for each search, the value of every move that was best at some iteration,
  from a `go searchmoves` search at the trace's full budget. This gives the cost, in centipawns, of
  stopping on an earlier move. It is Fork judging Fork with a deeper search, the form the KB
  records as confirmed by games in one other program (RSPSA's move score, Lines of Action).
- **Splits, by game and by opening:** train (shown to agents as stratum tables only), validation
  (selection only, never shown), lockbox (finalists only, once), and a fresh set generated after
  the search ends from a later SPRT run with a different opening origin.

### 6.2 The simulator and the fitness

- For each logged game, the simulator runs the candidate over the engine's own moves in order with
  a simulated clock (base and increment of the budget class; elapsed time = nodes / a fixed rate).
  The allocation rule sets each move's limits; the stop rule ends each search at an iteration; the
  engine's fixed rules (hard stop, forced-move stop, the iteration-growth rule, E1's
  partial-iteration rule) are modelled as the engine applies them.
- **Regret** of a move = the expected-score loss of the move at the stop against the reference
  move, through Fork's own fitted logistic (k = 65.1 cp for search scores at STC, calib-1112). A
  50 cp slip in a won position then costs little, as it should.
- **Fitness** = mean regret per game, lower is better, reported per budget class and per stratum
  (game phase, queens on or off, score band, stable or unstable search). A candidate must not be
  worse than the base in any budget class.
- **Reported in time-equivalents.** The base policy is also simulated at 0.8x to 1.25x of the
  clock. A candidate's regret is then read as "the base would need g% more time to match it", and
  1% of time is priced at 1.72 Elo (the in-situ speed-to-Elo fit). This is the unit the node-share
  fit already used.
- **Constants:** the evaluator fits each candidate's free coefficients on the train split by a
  deterministic coordinate search; validation scores the fitted candidate.
- **Behaviour signature:** the vector of per-stratum regrets, rounded. Two candidates with the same
  signature are the same policy; the second is not evaluated further (the pilot lost a fifth of its
  time to repeats).

### 6.3 What the simulator cannot see (stated before anyone relies on it)

1. **Open loop.** The positions come from games the base policy played. A candidate's own moves
   never change what happens next.
2. **Fixed carry-over.** Each logged search started from the hash table the replay left, not the
   one the candidate's own previous stop would have left.
3. **A fixed game length.** A policy could learn the length distribution of the logged games and
   empty its clock accordingly. Guard: training games are re-assembled with resampled lengths, and
   the reserve stays in the skeleton.
4. **The reference's horizon.** Regret is judged by a search four times deeper. Errors that need
   more than that (Fork sees a loss 9 plies late against SF19) are invisible.
5. **One thread, no pondering.**

These are why the simulator only ranks, and why stage A0 exists.

## 7. Search strategy

**Population.**
- Six islands, each seeded with the committed formula and each given a different family of signals
  to emphasise in its prompt: stability and share; score trend and fail-lows; search shape
  (selective depth, passes, node growth); clock economy (move number, increment, the opponent's
  clock); position type (material, queens, the static-to-search gap, lane B descriptors later);
  and one free explorer island asked for full replacements.
- Inside an island, candidates are grouped by behaviour signature (FunSearch's clusters). A parent
  group is drawn with a preference for better scores, and inside the group the shorter program is
  preferred.
- Every 5 generations (100 candidates per island) the worse half of the islands is reseeded from
  the best candidate of a surviving island. The period is a placeholder with this reasoning: an
  island needs enough candidates to move beyond its seed before it is judged.
- No MAP-Elites grid in the pilot. The signature clusters already keep variety, and the KB's
  methodology papers warn against machinery whose value is not measured. Two descriptors are
  logged for a later grid: the share of the clock spent in the first 20 moves, and the spread of
  time per move.

**How candidates are proposed.**
- One call returns four candidate bodies (FunSearch used four samples per prompt). No critic call
  per candidate: the critic existed to save a 12-minute evaluation, and the evaluation now costs
  seconds. The critic and the originality-checker review finalists only.
- Each prompt holds: the clean-room and hot-path rules; the region's signature and input list; two
  parents with their per-stratum regret tables; the idea ledger; the analyst's current notes; and
  one requested mutation type.
- Mutation types, drawn by fixed probabilities: change one term; add one signal; **remove or
  simplify one term**; combine two parents from different islands; full rewrite (explorer).
- The proposer writes no numbers. Coefficients are named and fitted by the evaluator.
- Model: the pilot used Sonnet. The published ablation that matters is that a single small model
  did worse than a mix (AlphaEvolve §4). Proposal: Sonnet for volume, a stronger model for the
  explorer island and the analyst. The owner's choice; the model of every call is logged.

**How failures feed back.** The pilot showed proposers the last 8 candidates, and old ideas came
back.
- **Dedupe** by behaviour signature before any further work.
- **An idea ledger:** one line per distinct signature group that was tried: a short tag, the
  stratum it helped or hurt, the verdict. It replaces the "last 8" window.
- **Stratum tables:** where the parent loses regret (for example unstable searches in queens-on
  middlegames). Proposals are aimed at a measured deficit.
- **The analyst** rewrites its notes every 5 generations from the whole ledger.
- **One repair attempt:** a gate failure returns the compiler's message once.
- **A fixed sign convention** in every prompt ("regret, lower is better", with a worked example).
  The pilot's proposers misread a negative reduction as a gain.

**The control arm.** 1,000 candidates from the same prompts with no parents, no ledger and no
analyst, through the same gate and screen. The comparison is the best-so-far curve against the
number of candidates, for the control, for each island and for the whole evolved population (the
seeds-by-iterations frontier the KB recommends reporting).

**Guards against overfitting the screen.**

| Risk | Guard |
|---|---|
| Fitting the training games | Selection on the validation split; the lockbox once; a fresh set from later games with other openings |
| The winner's curse | The split that selects never reports the size. The reported size is the lockbox's and then the SPRT's, corrected with `tools/gauntlet/stopbias.py` |
| Simulator exploits (game length, the trace's end, the fixed rate) | Resampled game lengths; a search that would run past its trace counts as a hard-limit stop with the worst label; every outlier gain is treated as an exploit until the full gate and a game stage say otherwise (KB: Oved et al.) |
| Reading the future | The harness passes signals up to the current iteration only; a planted clairvoyant candidate must be refused in the simulator's own tests |
| Over-engineered winners | Ablation to the minimal core at A3 (KB: Li et al. 2602.16928); a size limit; shorter programs preferred |
| Placebo signal | A policy that only spends a fixed share more or less must score zero in time-equivalents; a candidate's gain must survive shuffling its new signal across searches |
| Reproduced formulas and values | No numbers from the model; the name and constant screens on every finalist before any commit; a natural value that matches is credited and stays a candidate (owner, 2026-11-11) |
| Agent-reported strength | Never used (KB: self-measured Elo ran 200-1,100 too high) |

## 8. Lane B in brief: the blind-spot miner

One search, two consumers. Candidates are readable descriptors of a position. The screen asks how
much of the gap between Fork's score and the game's result a descriptor explains.

**The corpus** (all Fork's own, already on disk): the calibration study's positions with search
scores and results (2,638,939 from 33,600 STC games; the LTC and audit sets), the 20,000-node
sample (1,206,619 positions), the UHO diagnostic sets as report-only checks (they were chosen with
SF19's scores, so they are never a fitness).

**The screen.** Fit the result against the score with one scale, then with one scale per
descriptor class; score the held-out gain in log-loss with a penalty per class. Required of every
candidate:
- the same sign in the 20,000-node data and in the STC and LTC games (the gap grows with depth:
  class residuals of -1.5 to -2.3 at 20,000 nodes against -4.5 to -6 at STC);
- a gain that a permuted copy of the descriptor and a constant shift cannot reproduce (the
  correction tables' ranking failed exactly this);
- for a row-choice state: enough positions per (piece, square, state), and a low flip rate per
  move, from a cost model built on round 3's measurements.

**Stages and kill rules.**

| Stage | What | Cost *(estimate)* | Kill rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| B0 | The screen against known results: it must reproduce the queens-on class effect on held-out games; show a gain for pawn-structure descriptors on games played by a net from before pawn links (the one input family that paid on the clock: +46.09 at fixed nodes, +32.67 STC); and show none for a permuted placebo. The second check needs old games with their scores (UNVERIFIED that enough are kept) | CPU-hours; no tokens | It does not: lane B stops before any search |
| B1 | The search, as §7, about 3,000 candidates | About 15 CPU-h; tokens capped at 4 M | As A2 |
| B2 (calibration layer) | Only if idea 180's hand rescale gained. The best 3 class definitions: a fixed-node screen each, speed checked with `bench/ab.py`; the best one to design R at STC, then LTC | About 2 GitHub-h of screens, then at most 8 STC and 6 LTC stages | No screen above +5 Elo; H0 |
| B3 (row states) | The best 2 state sets beside idea 175's hand tier A and its control, which are planned anyway: the zero-delta speed prototype (175's own rule: killed below 0.95), then 2 seeds each, a speed-corrected fixed-node screen, the network SPRT for the best | About 9 GPU-h beyond 175's own arms; about 3 GitHub-h of screens; at most 13 STC stages | Speed below 0.95; both seeds below the hand tier A arm; H0 |

Lane B's first deliverable is useful even if nothing is adopted: a ranked list of where the
network's scores disagree with results, in readable terms, which also feeds lane A's position-type
island and the data-slice decisions.

## 11. Prior art and what would be new

**From the knowledge base (verified there on 2026-10-26 and 2026-10-27).**

| Work | What it is | What this plan takes from it |
|---|---|---|
| FunSearch (Romera-Paredes et al., Nature 625, 2024) | An LLM and an evaluator evolve one function inside a fixed skeleton; islands with resets; clusters by score signature; shorter programs preferred | The skeleton, the islands and reset, the signature clusters, four samples per prompt. Its scope note (efficient evaluator, rich scores) is the reason for choosing time management |
| AlphaEvolve (Novikov et al., arXiv 2506.13131) | Marked evolve blocks, diffs or rewrites, an evaluation cascade, several metrics. Its scheduler case evolved a heuristic on a simulator of past workloads and confirmed it in production | The cascade; a simulator on logged data confirmed by the real metric; per-stratum scores in prompts |
| MAP-Elites (Mouret and Clune, arXiv 1504.04909) | One elite per cell of a behaviour space. The paper suggests chess programs as an example but did not run it | Behaviour descriptors, logged for later |
| Björnsson and Marsland 2002; David-Tabibi et al. 2010 and 2014 | Search parameters tuned or evolved on node counts over a test suite; the 2010 match check was 300 games, +6 Elo | A warning: a suite fitness with an underpowered match shows nothing |
| Hauptman and Sipper 2007 | Genetic programming of mate-search programs; evolved trees were hard to analyse | Short, readable candidates |
| Kocsis, Szepesvári and Winands 2006 (RSPSA) | Move quality judged by the program's own deeper search, confirmed by games in Lines of Action; common random numbers | The reference labels; shared openings across arms |
| Pratola and Wolf 2003 | A cheap no-search fitness did not carry over to test sets | Screens must be validated |
| Li et al. 2026 (arXiv 2609.33115) | AlphaEvolve evolving game-search code: about 6,400 candidates per run, confirmation on fresh seeds, a measured noise floor, size and syntax gates. Against Stockfish its chess result was 0% at two of three settings | Scale; fresh-data confirmation; the noise floor as a stop rule |
| Li et al. 2026 (arXiv 2602.16928) | Evolved algorithms were over-engineered; ablation found a small core | Ablation of every finalist |
| Li et al. 2026 (FAMOU, arXiv 2606.10389) | A 3-game score correlated with a 20-40-game score at Spearman 0.11 | Cheap game screens rank poorly |
| Eberhardinger et al. 2024/2025 | Restarts beat longer runs; the first heuristic was often the final winner | Islands as restarts; the control arm |
| Gideoni, Risi and Gal 2026 | Simple baselines matched code evolution; the search space and prompt hints mattered more; noisy selection lost to baselines | The control arm; inputs as part of the experiment |
| Oved et al. 2026 | Rankings flip between one seed and forty; an outlier gain was an exploit | Several islands; outliers treated as exploits |
| Acher and Jézéquel 2026 | Agents' own Elo estimates ran 200-1,100 too high; a community constant was reproduced exactly | No agent-reported strength; no numbers from the model |
| Markovitch and Sella 1993/1996 (KB time-management §4.7) | Learned time allocation in checkers; recovered about a third of the possible gain | The headroom rule of A1 |
| Baier and Winands 2016 (KB time-management §6) | Control players that reproduce only a strategy's average time per move number | A6's time-shift control |

The KB's headline still stands: no primary source was found that runs an LLM evolution loop on an
alpha-beta chess engine's heuristics and reports SPRT-measured Elo. **UNVERIFIED** that none exists.

**Not in the KB, so UNVERIFIED and from memory only.** A kb-researcher pass should check each
before a declaration is written: LLM-written reward functions refined by evolutionary search
(Eureka, Ma et al. 2023); LLM-proposed features for tabular models (CAAFE, Hollmann et al. 2023);
symbolic regression tools; the Texel tuning method (already listed in ideas.md 152 as "to be
fetched"); Kocsis, Uiterwijk and van den Herik (2000) on learned time allocation (the KB itself
marks it unopened); and whether anyone has fitted time management from logged iteration traces
outside Fork.

**What would be new in Fork's version** (each claim of absence is UNVERIFIED beyond the KB):

1. **A time policy evolved on a replay simulator that is calibrated in time-equivalents and
   checked against the engine's own SPRT results before use.** The published loops either score
   candidates directly or, in games, by playing.
2. **Structure from the model, constants from measurement.** None of the three method papers has
   this split or the residual mode (KB: "not found in the source").
3. **The screen's validity measured as a first-class result**, with arms the screen predicts to
   lose. The KB found no proxy-against-final-metric study in any of the method papers apart from
   one production confirmation.
4. **Time allocation driven by the engine's own measured blind spots**, and the same descriptors
   reused as row choices for the network.
5. **The first SPRT-measured result of such a loop on an alpha-beta engine**, including the cost
   per adopted Elo, whichever way it comes out.

## 13. Risks I cannot remove

- **The simulator may pass A0 and still mislead on new kinds of policy.** A0's known results are
  all close relatives of variant (b). A4 is the only test on fresh candidates, and it resolves
  group differences of about 6 Elo, not single candidates.
- **The remaining headroom may be in what the simulator cannot see** (the collapse horizon,
  multi-thread behaviour at TCEC depth).
- **Time management goes stale too.** On re-measurement the stability cap moved from 72 to 32,
  though the fitted curve stayed within about 6%. An adopted structure needs its coefficients
  re-fitted on each new net and search, which the simulator makes cheap, and the re-fit still needs
  a game test.
- **Single-thread results.** Every Phase 4 Elo figure is one thread. A policy adopted now is
  re-checked after Lazy SMP.
- **Lane B's screen is a loss-type measure**, and loss has not predicted strength here. B0 may
  stop it.
- **My probabilities are judgements.** There is no prior hit rate for this method in this engine:
  both earlier runs (the LMR validation run and the ordering pilot) found nothing.

## 14. Decisions for the owner

1. **Target order:** time management first, row-choice states second, the calibration layer third
   and only if idea 180 gains. Or a different order.
2. **Coefficient fitting on the simulator.** Genetic and pattern-search methods are not adopted for
   parameter tuning (ideas.md 152). Fitting a candidate's coefficients on a deterministic offline
   objective is closer to idea 152's Texel-style fitting, whose scope today is small evaluation
   add-ons. This plan needs that scope extended to time-policy candidates, with fitted values kept
   as placeholders until games.
3. **The 12-arm batch of one-stage screens (A4).** Sequential halving was rated "only if a genuine
   batch of independent arms exists"; this is such a batch. Or the leaner 8-arm version.
4. **Caps:** 8 M tokens and 4,000 candidates for lane A; 4 M and 3,000 for lane B's search.
5. **Models** for volume proposals and for the explorer and analyst.
6. **Timing.** Step 20 of the Phase 4 order places idea 23 after the search features and the final
   retune. The offline parts (corpus, simulator, A0, A1) use no GitHub time and could start
   earlier; the search and the games should run on the final search and net, and time management
   is best judged after Lazy SMP. The corpus is rebuilt once on that engine.
7. **Where the trace corpus is built:** the cloud worker's class b queue, or local threads at night.
8. **A kb-researcher pass** on the UNVERIFIED prior art of §11 before any declaration.

If the answer is go, the next step is a declaration in the pilot's form (what is measured, how a
candidate is refused, what is reported, every threshold), committed before anything runs.
